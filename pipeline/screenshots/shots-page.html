The water under Cantrell Ridge

Five years of dye tracing have produced the first working map of the aquifer under Cantrell Ridge.

Elena Hart, 14 March 2026

Rain that falls on the limestone of Cantrell Ridge enters a subterranean drainage network within hours. Its extent has never been measured. A team from the state geological survey has spent five years following the water through it.

The aquifer ignores the county line. Dye released at the Fell Gap sink reappeared eleven kilometres north, well inside the territory of a different water authority. Two agencies draw on one body of water. Until this survey neither had a figure for how much of it there is.

We were managing the reservoir we could see. The larger store sits beneath it, and it does not refill on the schedule anyone assumed.

Reading the calcite

Dating the passage is harder. Flowstone yields absolute ages from uranium-thorium measurements of calcite laid down in standing water. The oldest sample from Gallery Four returned an age of 214,000 years. That figure puts a floor under the age of the passage itself, because the calcite could not form until the passage had drained.

Gallery Four is beautiful and almost undisturbed, which is part of the difficulty. The survey withheld its entrance coordinates from the published map. Visitor traffic has damaged comparable chambers elsewhere in the county within a single season.

Not every hydrologist accepts the boundaries the map draws. The dye results establish that water moves between the northern and southern sections. The volume is inferred rather than gauged, and a second tracing run is planned for the spring.

The county has already changed one practice. Withdrawal permits near the ridge are now reviewed against the recharge estimate instead of against surface flow alone.

The map will be revised. A survey of this kind is provisional by nature, and the passages that carry the most water are usually the ones nobody has entered.
